How it works.

California, November 2000 to August 2026.

What the numbers mean

Satellites record a hotspot when a pixel is hotter than its surroundings, usually because of a fire. Two families of instruments do this. MODIS (on the Terra and Aqua satellites, since 2000 and 2002) has 1 km pixels. VIIRS (on S-NPP since 2012, NOAA-20 since 2018 and NOAA-21 since 2023) has 375 m pixels and sees many more, smaller fires. Adding the two together would make fire activity seem to jump in 2012 and again when each new satellite started.

Every count on these pages, the Latest page included, is therefore given on one scale: MODIS-equivalent hotspots, the number of detections Terra and Aqua together would have made. It is a measure of fire activity, not of burned area or of the number of separate fires. One large fire produces many hotspots, over many days.

Data and processing

  1. Source. NASA FIRMS standard-processing archive: MODIS Collection 6.1 and VIIRS 375 m (S-NPP, NOAA-20, NOAA-21). Near-real-time data is not used, so the record ends where the archive ends (31 August 2026 for MODIS, 31 July 2026 for VIIRS when downloaded).
  2. Cleaning. Exact duplicate rows are removed. Only vegetation fires (type 0) are kept, so volcanoes, industrial heat sources and offshore flares are dropped. Low-confidence detections are dropped (MODIS confidence below 30, VIIRS "low").
  3. Area. Detections are clipped to California's outline (Natural Earth) and placed on a 0.25° grid (about 25 km). A drawn area on the map is made of whole grid cells.
  4. Harmonization turns the detections into one MODIS-equivalent count per day, month and grid cell (next section).
  5. Checks. Every build is checked automatically: no detection counted twice, each detection's weight adds up exactly to the published count, and the published files match the data contract.

How MODIS and VIIRS are made comparable

  • Before 20 January 2012 the count is the observed MODIS count (Terra + Aqua).
  • Terra-only months (November 2000 to 3 July 2002, before Aqua flew) are multiplied by 2.056, the Terra + Aqua ÷ Terra ratio measured in California in 2003–2010.
  • From 20 January 2012 the count is the average of two estimates: the observed MODIS count, and the S-NPP VIIRS count multiplied by a factor k for that calendar month. For California, k is between 0.22 (September) and 0.35 (March): roughly one MODIS hotspot for every three to five VIIRS hotspots. k was learned from 2014–2018, when both sensors were working.
  • Only one VIIRS satellite (S-NPP) is used for the conversion, so the start of NOAA-20 and NOAA-21 does not add fake activity. NOAA-20 stands in for S-NPP only on days when S-NPP has no data.
  • Satellite outages are missing data, not zero fires. On days when one satellite delivered nothing, the estimate uses the others. Days with no data at all are shown as "no data" (hatched in the calendar), never as zero.
  • Every detection carries a weight (harmonized_weight in the hotspot CSV). The weights of a day's detections add up to that day's harmonized count.

How well it works

Six methods were compared. Each was fitted on 2014–2018 and scored on 2019–2023, years it had never seen, by converting S-NPP counts and comparing them with real MODIS counts month by month. The chosen method (a ratio per calendar month, version A1-1.0) scored best in California:

California in brief

Fire activity in California is concentrated in summer and autumn: July to October hold 78% of all harmonized hotspots in 2003–2025, and August alone 30%. The most active years in the record are 2020 (about 48,400 hotspots) and 2021 (about 42,800), more than twice any other year. They match the 2020 season, the largest in the state's modern record at over 4.2 million acres including the August Complex, and the 2021 Dixie Fire, the largest single fire in California history (963,309 acres). The next years, 2008 and 2018, match the June 2008 lightning siege (over 1.1 million acres) and the 2018 Mendocino Complex. The quietest full years are 2010 and 2011.

Sources: CAL FIRE 2020 incident archive; Dixie Fire; Bay Nature, "Wildfire in California" (2008); Mendocino Complex Fire. Counts are from this site's data; the checks are in docs/validation/S26/README.md.

Known limits

  • The range is approximate. It should hold about 80% of months; in the test years it held 78% in California (fewer in two other tested regions). It is not a statistical confidence interval.
  • The error grows after 2023. On 2024 to July 2026 the typical error was 20% and the conversion ran 10% low. A possible reason, not verified, is the orbit drift of Terra and Aqua.
  • Terra-only months (November 2000 to June 2002) are less certain. They are scaled from one satellite (typical error about 9% when tested on 2011–2018).
  • Small numbers are less reliable. Months, days or drawn areas with few hotspots have larger relative errors. The conversion is fitted to California as a whole; it is not tuned for single grid cells.
  • Hotspots are not fires or burned area. Clouds and thick smoke hide fires; small or short fires between satellite passes are missed; one large fire gives many hotspots.
  • Drawn areas use whole months. Their calendar, charts and comparison come from the monthly grid. The hotspot CSV of an area uses exact dates.
  • No near-real-time data. The newest data is from the standard archive, a few months behind. S-NPP stopped on 2 November 2026, and Terra and Aqua are due to stop in late 2026 or early 2027, so extending the record will need a new reference.
  • Only California was checked in this version. The factors cannot be used for other places without refitting.

The Latest page and the drawn area

The Latest page is the day-by-day calendar designed by Yashneil Gajjala (yashneil75/califire, MIT licence). Its layout is his; its numbers are ours: the harmonized daily count described above.

  • All of California: each day is the daily harmonized count. Days with no satellite data are hatched, never zero.
  • A drawn area: each day is the sum of the harmonized weights of the hotspots inside the area (they add up exactly to the day's count). Rankings against other years ("more active than X% of August days") and the 24-month strongest days are given for all of California only, because they need every day of the record; an area shows the strongest days of the month on screen.
  • Day and night: the split of a day's count between daytime and night-time detections, from the same weights.
  • The same rule applies on every page: once you draw an area on the Map, the Latest page, Overview, Archive, Map, comparison and downloads all show that area only. Clear it to go back to all of California.

Experimental forecast

The purple days on the Latest page are a forecast made with Yashneil's method: Mistral's Timestral-5M time-series model (revision bdd20cf), given the last 730 days of the harmonized daily count (log scale, days without data left out) and asked for the next 7 days, with a median and a 10th–90th percentile range. No weather or fire information is used. It is a forecast of satellite-detected activity, not of fire risk, and must not be used for safety decisions.

Which days it covers: our data ends on 31 August 2026 (the standard archive ends there), so the forecast covers 1–7 September 2026, not the days after today. It is shown for all of California only.
